    Section Appendix — Fallback Plan

    Facilitator reference. The timeline above assumes the workshop runs as designed — everyone gets online, everyone’s pretext.plus account works, and the editor behaves. This appendix is what to do if that isn’t true.
    🔗
    The goal of the fallback is not to replicate the full hands-on experience. It’s to preserve the core takeaway: participants leave understanding what PreTeXt authoring looks and feels like, and with a concrete sense of what they’d write if they tried it themselves.
    🔗

    Subsection When to switch to fallback mode

    Switch — fully or partially — if any of these happen:
    🔗
    • The venue Wi-Fi is down or throttled badly enough that pretext.plus won’t load for most of the room.
      🔗
    • The pretext.plus service is slow, broken, or rejecting sign-ups.
      🔗
    • An institutional firewall is blocking account confirmation emails.
      🔗
    • More than about a quarter of the room can’t get their editor working after ten minutes of troubleshooting.
      🔗
    🔗
    Don’t switch the whole room if only two or three people are stuck — pair them with neighbors who are working and keep going. Switch the whole room if the pattern is clearly environmental.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Graceful degradation — three levels

    Level 1 — Pair up
    🔗
    Use when: a minority of the room is offline or account-blocked.
    🔗
    Ask each stuck participant to pair with a neighbor who has a working editor. The partner drives; the stuck participant reads aloud the snippet to paste and decides what to customize. Everyone still sees the Edit → Rebuild rhythm and leaves with a finished lesson associated with at least one of them.
    🔗
    Level 2 — Instructor-driven demo with paper exercises
    🔗
    Use when: most of the room cannot edit, but the projector still works.
    🔗
    The facilitator drives a single pretext.plus project on the projector. Work through the five Guided Practice exercises live, one at a time, with the room calling out small edits. Distribute the printed participant handout so people can annotate each snippet by hand, writing in the changes they’d make. For Build a Lesson, hand out the two starter options on paper and ask participants to mark them up with their own topic — title changes, example swaps, reworded exercises. Collect a few at the end and project them as the Show and Tell.
    🔗
    This level still preserves the core: participants see source, see preview, see a cross-reference resolve, and produce something concrete.
    🔗
    Level 3 — Fully offline read-and-compare
    🔗
    Use when: nothing online is usable, not even for the facilitator.
    🔗
    Distribute the prebuilt offline packet (see the next subsection). Walk through the “source on the left, output on the right” comparison sheets, then have participants edit a printed starter lesson in pencil and share with a neighbor. The session still ends on time and participants leave with a realistic mental model of the workflow.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Offline packet to have on hand

    Bring the following in printed form (or on a local USB drive) before the session starts. This is cheap insurance.
    🔗
    1. Screenshots of the editor. The pretext.plus landing page, an empty project, a project with source on the left and preview on the right, and the Sharable link dialog. Enough for someone who has never seen the tool to recognize what’s happening.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Side-by-side “source vs. output” sheets for each of the five Guided Practice exercises. Left column: the PreTeXt snippet. Right column: a screenshot of the rendered result.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Printed starter lessons. Both Build-a-Lesson starters, printed with generous margins so participants can annotate in pencil.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. A prebuilt sample HTML output on a USB drive or local file that the facilitator can open without internet, so there’s always something real to show on the projector.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. This hub document as a PDF, so participants can follow the timeline even without network access.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection A non-deployment completion path

    The default “done” signal is a sharable link. If sharing is broken, substitute any of these:
    🔗
    • A screenshot of the participant’s preview panel, sent to the facilitator or a neighbor.
      🔗
    • A printed marked-up starter lesson handed in at the end.
      🔗
    • A read-aloud of one thing they’d add if they had another hour — a lightweight oral “Show and Tell.”
      🔗
    🔗
    Whatever form it takes, each participant should leave having produced something concrete they could point at and say “I made that.” That’s the contract.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Pacing adjustments

    If you’ve switched to Level 2 or 3, the timeline still holds roughly, but cut the following first:
    🔗
    • Interactive Feature Gallery (4:10-4:20) — compress to three favorites, shown on projector from the prebuilt HTML.
      🔗
    • Finished-early prompts in Guided Practice — drop entirely.
      🔗
    • Show and Tell (5:40-5:50) — shorten to one lesson or one collected paper starter, and give the extra minutes back to Build-a-Lesson.
      🔗
    🔗
    Protect the Build-a-Lesson phase. That’s where the core learning happens, and it’s the last thing to cut.
    🔗
